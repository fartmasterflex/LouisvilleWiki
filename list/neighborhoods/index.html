Neighborhoods

A historical guide to the distinct pockets that make up the city.

Open the interactive version →
  • Downtown / Central Business District · Search ↗

    The original 1778 settlement at the Falls of the Ohio. Grew from a rugged river outpost to a massive commercial and shipping hub by the 1850s. Home to the second largest collection of cast-iron facades in the US (after SoHo) along West Main Street.

  • Old Louisville · Search ↗

    Originally a suburb called the 'Southern Extension'. Built primarily between 1880 and 1910, fueled by the 1883 Southern Exposition. Home to the largest contiguous collection of Victorian mansions in the US, and the third largest historic preservation district overall.

  • The Highlands · Search ↗

    Stretching along the Bardstown Road corridor. Earliest settlements post-date the original city limits, with major residential development beginning in the 1890s following the extension of streetcar lines. Grew as a string of streetcar suburbs (Cherokee Triangle, Bonnycastle, Tyler Park) rather than one planned neighborhood.

  • Germantown / Schnitzelburg · Search ↗

    Settled in the mid-19th century by German butchers, tradesmen, and brewers who formed tight-knit enclaves. Shotgun houses were built closely together around local parishes like St. Therese and St. Elizabeth. Continues the legacy of neighborhood taverns.

  • Portland · Search ↗

    Established in 1811 as a separate, rival river port town below the falls. Annexed by Louisville in 1837, split off again in 1842, and annexed for good in 1852. French, Irish, and German immigrants settled here, working on the river and the Portland Canal. Boasts deep maritime heritage.

  • Butchertown · Search ↗

    Founded in the 1830s as the epicenter of Louisville's meatpacking industry due to its location along Beargrass Creek. Fueled heavily by German and Irish immigrants, shaped by the stockyards and slaughterhouses that dominated its economy for a century.

  • NuLu (East Market District) · Search ↗

    Historically a commercial and industrial corridor serving the meatpacking plants of Butchertown, and later a hub for auto shops. Rebranded as NuLu in the 2000s, though its brick facades and warehouses root it firmly in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

  • Clifton / Crescent Hill · Search ↗

    Grew in the late 19th century along the Frankfort Turnpike. Clifton housed workers from the nearby Bourbon Stock Yards, while Crescent Hill, featuring the 1879 Crescent Hill Reservoir, became a heavily forested streetcar suburb for wealthier residents.

  • Smoketown · Search ↗

    Established in the 1860s, it is the only Louisville neighborhood with a continuous history of being settled by Black Americans since the Civil War. It became a thriving hub for Black-owned businesses, jazz clubs, and schools in the early 20th century.

  • West End (Russell, Shawnee, Chickasaw) · Search ↗

    Initially developed as affluent streetcar suburbs in the late 19th century. Russell became known as 'Louisville's Harlem', lined with Black-owned businesses and jazz clubs along Walnut Street (now Muhammad Ali Blvd) until mid-century urban renewal policies decimated the corridor.

  • St. Matthews · Search ↗

    Originally a farming community known as Gilman's Point in the late 1700s. Developed into a major agricultural center known for potato farming in the early 20th century (via the St. Matthews Produce Exchange) before suburbanizing rapidly after the 1937 flood.

  • Jeffersontown (J-Town) · Search ↗

    Founded in 1797, making it one of the oldest settlements in Jefferson County. It originated as a rural farming community and stagecoach stop before transitioning into a major suburban and industrial hub in the mid-20th century.

  • Beechmont / South End · Search ↗

    Founded in 1892 as a summer neighborhood for wealthy Louisvillians escaping the crowded city, named for its beech trees and linked by streetcar in 1900. Olmsted's Iroquois Park made it a desirable suburb; Craftsman houses still line its streets.

  • Shelby Park · Search ↗

    A late-1800s streetcar neighborhood south of downtown, built around its early-1900s Olmsted-firm park. Logan Street Market and a wave of new restaurants have made it one of the city's busiest corridors.

  • Paristown Pointe · Search ↗

    A tiny pocket between Germantown and Phoenix Hill, long home to the Louisville Stoneware works. Now anchored by Paristown Hall, Louisville Stoneware, and the Village Market food hall.

  • Phoenix Hill · Search ↗

    Rising just east of downtown, it was a German brewing district; the Phoenix Brewery and its hilltop beer park gave it the name. It borders the downtown medical campus.

  • Irish Hill · Search ↗

    Settled by Irish immigrants in the mid-1800s along Beargrass Creek and Lexington Rd, with homes clustered near the old rail lines. Small, hilly, and full of shotgun houses.

  • Limerick · Search ↗

    A small Old Louisville-adjacent neighborhood settled by Irish railroad workers in the 1860s-80s, anchored by St. Louis Bertrand Church.

  • Cherokee Triangle · Search ↗

    A Victorian and early-1900s streetcar suburb between Bardstown Rd and Cherokee Park, laid out in the 1870s-90s. Home of the Daniel Boone statue and the Cherokee Triangle Art Fair.

  • Deer Park / Belknap / Douglass Loop · Search ↗

    Early-1900s streetcar suburbs south of the Highlands. The Douglass Loop, where the streetcar turned around, is now a busy little restaurant and farmers market hub.

  • Audubon Park · Search ↗

    A separate small city founded in the 1910s as a planned garden suburb. Its streets are named for birds, and it's known for its spring dogwoods.

  • Shively · Search ↗

    Its own city since 1938, on the southwest edge. Built on the distilling trade along the old Dixie Highway, home to the historic Stitzel-Weller distillery.

  • Newburg · Search ↗

    One of the oldest Black communities in the county, settled by freed families in the 1800s (originally called Petersburg). The Newburg Library anchors it today.

  • Highland Park · Search ↗

    A working-class neighborhood next to the airport, largely bought out and demolished in the 1990s for the airport's expansion.

  • Fern Creek / Okolona / Valley Station · Search ↗

    Former farm crossroads and railroad stops that boomed into suburbs after WWII. Now home to much of the city's immigrant food scene and a new Fern Creek library (2027).

  • Prospect / Anchorage / Norton Commons · Search ↗

    Old East End estates and railroad-suburb towns (Anchorage's tree-lined lanes date to the 1870s), plus Norton Commons, a 2000s 'new urbanist' walkable town.
